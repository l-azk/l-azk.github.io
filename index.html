Shadows & Objecthood

Perception & Cognition Lab · UBC

July 2025–present

What is the relationship between shadows and their objects in visual processing?

Shadows are weird! They carry useful information about the objects that cast them, but they can change shape, stretch across different surfaces, and even become physically separated from their objects. This raises a basic question: does the visual system bind a shadow to the object that casts it?

To test this, we measure how easily attention moves between objects, shadows, and surrounding surfaces. Across experiments, we find that attention moves between a shadow and its casting object as if the usual boundary between separate objects is absent, even when the shadow is distorted or physically separated from its object. We suggest that shadows may therefore be : distinct from their casting objects, but nevertheless bound together with them in visual processing.

AI & Romantic Relationships

Social Cognition & Emotion Lab · UBC

August 2026–present

What happens when you chat with AI about a disagreement with your partner?

People increasingly turn to AI not only for information, but also for advice about situations involving other people. This raises a different question from whether an AI response is simply accurate or useful: can the perspective an AI provides change how we understand someone close to us?

In this project, participants describe a disagreement with their romantic partner and then discuss that disagreement with an AI chatbot. We compare conversations in which the chatbot supports the participant’s perspective with those in which it encourages them to consider their partner’s perspective. The broader goal is to understand which type of AI response is more helpful when people turn to AI to navigate interpersonal conflict.

AI & Apologies

Social Cognition & Emotion Lab · UBC

August 2026–present

If AI writes the better apology, would you actually use it?

Generative AI can produce polished interpersonal messages almost instantly. But an apology is not judged only by how well it is written. Its value may also depend on whether it reflects the apologizer’s own thoughts, effort, and intentions.

In this project, I study the tension between quality and authorship. When people compare an apology they wrote themselves with one generated by AI, what determines which message they are willing to send? This provides a way to examine how AI changes judgments of authenticity: an AI can produce a polished, well-worded apology, but that same apology may feel less sincere because it was not written by the person sending it.

Seeing Correlation

Visual Cognition Lab · UBC

March 2025–present

How do we perceive correlation?

A scatterplot can represent the same statistical relationship while looking very different depending on how its individual points are rendered. We examine whether changing low-level visual properties, such as the size, shape, and colour of the dots, changes how people perceive correlation.

Our findings so far suggest that perceived correlation is remarkably robust to these changes. Despite differences in the appearance of individual elements, observers recover similar estimates of the higher-order relationship between them. This suggests that the visual system can extract abstract statistical structure while remaining relatively insensitive to variation in the features that make up the display.
