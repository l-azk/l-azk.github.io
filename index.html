About

I study visual cognition and human–AI interaction.

I’m a fourth-year Psychology student at the University of British Columbia. I’m interested in how the mind organizes what we see, and in what happens when AI becomes part of the way we think, communicate, and make decisions.

More broadly, I’m drawn to questions about how people make sense of information and how the tools we use can shape that process. My work currently approaches these questions from both visual cognition and human–AI interaction.

Beyond research, I spend much of my time training for triathlons, mountain biking, and snowboarding. Lately, I’ve also been getting interested in shooting little moments on a camcorder and learning how to edit them into short videos.

Shadows & Objecthood

What counts as part of an object?

A cast shadow is caused by an object and moves with it, yet visually it often appears to be something separate: a dark region lying across another surface. This makes shadows a useful case for asking how the visual system decides where one object ends and something else begins.

My work asks whether attention treats a cast shadow as part of its casting object, as an independent object-like region, or as something in between. I approach this using object-based attention paradigms that let us test whether perceptual processing crosses the apparent boundary between an object and its shadow.

AI & Romantic
Relationships

What happens when AI becomes a third voice in a disagreement?

People increasingly turn to AI not only for information, but also for advice about situations involving other people. That raises a different kind of question from whether an AI response is simply accurate or useful: can the perspective an AI provides change the way we understand someone close to us?

In this project, I study conflict between romantic partners and compare AI feedback that supports a person’s existing interpretation with feedback that challenges it. The broader goal is to understand whether AI can shift perspective-taking, emotional responses, and the way people subsequently approach interpersonal disagreement.

AI &
Apologies

If AI writes the better apology, would you actually use it?

Generative AI can produce polished interpersonal messages almost instantly. But an apology is not judged only by how well it is written. Its value can also depend on whether the recipient believes it reflects the apologizer’s own thoughts, effort, and intentions.

I’m interested in the tension between quality and authorship: when people compare an apology they wrote themselves with one generated by AI, what determines which message they are willing to send? This provides a way to study how AI changes judgments of authenticity in communication where sincerity matters.

Visual Cognition Lab · UBC

March 2025–present

Visual cognition · Information perception

Seeing
Correlation

How much does the appearance of data change the relationship we think we see?

A scatterplot may represent exactly the same statistical relationship while looking very different depending on how its points are rendered. Changes in size, shape, luminance, or colour can therefore alter the visual evidence available to an observer even when the underlying data remain fixed.

This work examines how low-level visual properties influence perceived correlation. More broadly, it asks how seemingly incidental design choices affect the quantitative judgments people make from visual displays.
